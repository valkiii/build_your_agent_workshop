Facilitator prep · hands-on exercise

You vs. Gemma

Groups of five judge four real articles against one interest prompt by hand, then run the exact same prompt through Gemma and see where they agree and where they don't. Tested end-to-end against the live pipeline — the results below are real model output, not a guess.

The interest prompt

Deliberately in the same two-paragraph shape as the app's real prompts/interest.txt — "what I want" then "what to reject" — so it drops straight into the Streamlit box or the file with no rewriting. Focus/attention as the topic is a small joke worth saying out loud: a distraction-free curator, judging articles about distraction.

Hand this out, or paste into the app

I want practical, evidence-backed articles about focus, attention, and deep work — things a busy person could actually try this week. Prefer real research or genuine lived experience behind the claims, concrete techniques over vague advice.

Reject generic productivity-app marketing and listicles, opinion pieces with no real evidence or experience behind them, and anything not actually about focus or attention.

The four articles

Not four random links — a deliberate spread, picked so the room won't be unanimous in thirty seconds. One should be an easy approve, one an easy reject, and one built for disagreement. The fourth turned out to be the most useful of the lot for a reason I didn't plan — see the answer key below.

Facilitator answer key

Not a right-answer sheet to hand out — the point of B is that reasonable people disagree. Use this to steer the discussion, and to know in advance which one is going to run long.

A Approve

Should be the easy one. Named researcher, twenty years of cited methodology, specific mechanisms (the "hooks," "meta-awareness"), nothing being sold. If a group rejects this one, the discussion is about the prompt being read too strictly.

Gemma actually saidapprove — "The article presents empirical research on the impact of technology on attention spans." Tags: Attention Span, Technology Use, Focus Research.

B Coin flip — the real discussion

Built to be argued about. It's practical and clearly comes from real experience, which the prompt asks for — but it cites almost nothing, leaning on one unsourced "research has shown" line. Whether that clears "evidence-backed" is exactly the judgment call the whole workshop is about.

Gemma actually saidapprove — "The article directly addresses developing sustained focus through a trainable process." It read "trainable, specific, experience-backed" as satisfying the prompt and didn't push back on the missing citations. Ask the room: did every group agree?

C It broke the model — and that's the lesson

This is the one to watch live, not predict. The article is itself a numbered list of 13 apps. The summarizer was told "write a 2–3 sentence summary" and instead mirrored the input's own shape — it wrote a mini numbered list back. Handed that, the curator didn't produce one verdict; it produced four separate ones, one per app, each in its own JSON code fence — and approved every single one, including Reclaim's own product, reasoning it "focuses on using AI to manage scheduling to defend focus time." The one thing the prompt named by name to reject is exactly what got approved, by accident, because the format broke first.

Gemma actually said(malformed — four JSON blobs instead of one) "This addresses focus and attention management with a reference to scientific research" (on Freedom) … "This focuses on using AI to manage scheduling to defend focus time" (on Reclaim, the publisher's own product).

D Reject

The trap is that it's good. Well-sourced, well-written, genuinely interesting — and has nothing to do with focus or attention. It tests whether people (and the model) can tell "well-made" apart from "on-topic."

Gemma actually saidreject — "The article discusses the history of citizen science rather than focus, attention, or deep work." Clean, correct, no ambiguity.

Why C matters more than a clean four-for-four would have

I initially picked C to be the easy reject — a listicle, self-disclosed as the publisher's own product, exactly the category the prompt names. Running it live surfaced something better: because the source article is itself a numbered list, the small model's summary mirrored that structure instead of following the "2–3 sentences" instruction, and the curator step then mirrored the broken summary instead of forcing one clean JSON verdict — approving the publisher's own product along the way. That's not a mistake in the exercise. It's a live demonstration of exactly what the deck's "small models need explicit structure" slide is about, happening to an article the room is holding in their hands. I'd leave it in and narrate it rather than swap it for something that behaves.

Caveat: model output isn't fully deterministic — a re-run on the day could come out differently (cleaner, or differently broken). Worth a quick re-run yourself close to the date so you know what you're walking into.

Running it

Split into groups of five

20 participants → 4 groups. Mix coders and non-coders in each group if you can — the disagreement is more interesting across that line, not less.

Hand out the prompt and the four links, alone, 15 minutes

Everyone reads the prompt once, then reads (or skims) all four articles and privately marks each one approve or reject. No talking yet — the private call is what makes the group discussion afterward worth having.

Printed cards or a shared doc both work — just not a live shared spreadsheet, which lets people see each other's answers before they've made their own.

Groups compare and argue, 10 minutes

Each group reconciles into one verdict per article. The instruction that matters: they have to state why, in terms of the prompt's own words — not "I liked it," but "it doesn't cite real research" or "it's not actually about focus."

Run the same prompt through Gemma, 10 minutes

Non-coders: paste the prompt into the app's interest box, paste in the four URLs as sources, run it. Coders: same thing from main.py, or just re-use the live Ollama slide from the talk.

Compare, 5 minutes, whole room

Show of hands per article: who agreed with the model, who didn't. B is where this gets interesting — ask a group that approved it and a group that rejected it to both say why in one sentence.

Then: make it theirs

This is where the exercise hands off to the timetable's existing 10:45–11:15 "own build" slot — no new time needed, just content for time that's already there.

Once the room has seen where they agreed and disagreed with Gemma, the natural next move is theirs to make: rewrite the prompt so it would have caught what they wanted it to catch (tighten it if B should've been a reject, loosen it if it should've been an approve), then swap in a topic and a handful of articles or blogs they actually care about, and watch the same four-step judgment happen on something personal instead of a prompt I picked for them.

Two things worth deciding before this goes out

The timetable currently describes something else

The 9:50–10:15 slot is written as "non-coders watch the Streamlit app + Ollama chat walkthrough; coders walk the actual Python files" — a demo, not this silent-read exercise. The 10:15–10:30 slot ("you vs. the model") already matches steps 4–5 above exactly. Want me to update the timetable slide's wording to describe this exercise directly, or keep it generic and let your speaker notes carry the specifics?

Printed vs. on-screen for the read/decide step

Printing the four articles means no laptops open yet — closer to the physical-relay spirit at 9:35. Reading on-screen is faster to prep (no printer needed) and lets people click through to the source. Either works; I'd lean printed, purely because it keeps step 2 laptop-free and step 4 becomes the moment laptops open for the first time in Block A.
